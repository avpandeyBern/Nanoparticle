# Nanoparticle delivery of natural products in prostate cancer: a critical review of direct comparisons, exposure, tumor control and reporting

# Abstract

Nanoparticle delivery can improve the apparent potency of natural products in prostate cancer models, yet the size and the meaning of that advantage depend almost entirely on the comparison that produced it. This critical review evaluates formulated versus free natural products in prostate cancer, prioritizing experiments that match the active moiety, the active-equivalent dose, the route, the schedule, the disease stage and the endpoint. A targeted PubMed search through 8 October 2026 was combined with citation-led retrieval and with a verification pass in which every quantitative value reproduced here was checked against the primary full text wherever that text could be retrieved. The evidence is organized into matched single-compound experiments, unequal-dose experiments, combination treatments, pharmacokinetic studies and cell-only investigations, and is summarized in five data tables. Curcumin, epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) and tea catechins supply the most informative examples. Some matched animal comparisons genuinely favor formulation; several frequently cited EGCG experiments instead establish activity at a lower administered dose, which is a different and more limited claim. A nanoparticle-curcumin study in Pten-deficient mice reduced proliferation without reducing tumor burden and reported liver enlargement, illustrating why negative and off-target outcomes must be retained. Greater particle accumulation, higher total drug concentration and stronger in vitro potency do not by themselves establish greater pharmacologically available tumor exposure or a wider therapeutic index; in one intravenous curcumin dataset, a separate biodistribution experiment showed tumor exposure rising 4.22-fold as plasma exposure fell, a pattern consistent with redistribution rather than with more drug delivered. Dose-unit discrepancies, conflicting endpoint timing, incomplete variance reporting and heterogeneous models prevented a defensible pooled animal effect estimate, so results are presented as explicitly labeled within-study comparisons. Conference abstracts are discussed separately and are not treated as independent confirmatory experiments. Overall, formulation improves measured activity in selected experimental settings, but a general nanoparticle advantage, and clinical benefit, remain unproven. The decisive next studies should integrate dose-matched and exposure-matched comparisons, free-combination controls, a standard-of-care benchmark, blinded tumor measurement, repeated-dose toxicology and complete animal-level reporting.

**Keywords:** prostate cancer; nanoparticles; natural products; curcumin; epigallocatechin gallate; pharmacokinetics; androgen receptor; reporting bias

# Introduction

The relevant question in this field is not whether a nanoparticle carrying a natural product can inhibit a prostate cancer cell line or a xenograft. Almost any cytotoxic or antiproliferative payload, delivered in almost any carrier, will do that relative to an untreated control. The question that determines translational value is narrower: does formulation improve the performance of the *same* active compound under a comparison that isolates a clinically useful delivery advantage? That advantage can take several distinct forms, and the experiments needed to demonstrate each one differ.

The field's foundational result illustrates the distinction. The proof-of-principle study of nanoparticle EGCG reported tumor inhibition using one-tenth of the free-compound dose {Siddiqui, 2009 #1}, which established a rationale for *dose-sparing* investigation rather than a universal equal-dose effect. Curcumin later supplied matched-dose xenograft comparisons, including direct intratumoral administration, but such experiments deliberately bypass absorption and much of systemic disposition {Yallapu, 2014 #2}. These two early templates, a lower-dose comparison and a route-restricted comparison, already answer different questions, and conflating them has shaped much of what followed.

Four claims recur throughout this literature, and they are not interchangeable: greater potency at the same nominal concentration; greater tumor control at the same administered dose; preserved activity at a lower dose; and a better balance of efficacy against toxicity. None of these follows automatically from particle size, from encapsulation efficiency, or from a statistically significant contrast with untreated controls. This review focuses on phytochemicals and other investigational natural bioactives formulated in polymeric nanoparticles, lipid nanoparticles, liposomes, polymeric micelles, solid lipid and nanostructured lipid carriers, nanoemulsions and, where the carrier is itself pharmacologically active, metal and theranostic particles. Purely diagnostic particles, benign prostatic hyperplasia, prostatitis and non-prostate tumors do not supply direct prostate cancer efficacy evidence and are excluded. # The molecular landscape: targets and carriers

The natural products most studied as prostate cancer nanomedicines converge on a small set of pathways (Figure 2C). EGCG and other catechins modulate androgen receptor (AR) signaling and proliferative cascades; curcumin is a pleiotropic inhibitor of NF-κB-driven inflammation, of AR expression and of PI3K–AKT survival signaling, and its clinical development has been limited chiefly by poor oral bioavailability rather than by weak target activity {Anand, 2007 #38}. Resveratrol engages AKT and sirtuin biology, quercetin the PI3K axis, genistein the AR and NF-κB nodes, and apigenin the AR program. A distinct and underappreciated mechanism is steroidogenic: the curcumin–piperine combination modulates the cytochrome P450 enzymes CYP17A1 and CYP21A2 that control adrenal and intratumoral androgen synthesis {Yakubu, 2025 #30}. Because CYP17A1 is the validated target of abiraterone in castration-resistant disease {de Bono, 2011 #41}, a nanoparticle that genuinely reaches and inhibits this enzyme would act on a clinically established node rather than on a generic antiproliferative one. Figure 2D places this intervention point within the canonical androgen-synthesis pathway, drawn in the standard steroid scheme.

The carrier side of the landscape is equally heterogeneous. Polymeric particles built from poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) or polycaprolactone, liposomes, polymeric micelles, solid lipid and nanostructured lipid carriers, and nanoemulsions each impose different drug-loading, release and stability behavior, and each interacts differently with the mononuclear phagocyte system (Table 1). The premise that unites them, passive accumulation through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, derives from macromolecular tumor-tropism first described four decades ago {Matsumura, 1986 #36}. That premise is now known to be fragile. A cross-model analysis found that the paracellular gaps long assumed to admit nanoparticles account for a minority of delivery, and that up to ~97% of nanoparticle entry into solid tumors proceeds through an active, cell-mediated route {Sindhwani, 2020 #37}. EPR is also highly heterogeneous across tumor types, sites and stages. Figure 2B depicts the sequence of barriers, absorption, first-pass metabolism, circulation, reticuloendothelial uptake and extravasation, that separate an administered dose from a tumor cell, and shows how the three common administration routes each bypass a different subset of them.

# Review approach and the limits of this synthesis

A targeted PubMed search was run with a publication-date ceiling of 8 October 2026, combining prostate terms with nanoparticle, nanomicelle, nanocapsule, nanoformulation, nanochemoprevention and liposome terms and a panel of natural-product names. It returned 314 records, whose titles and abstracts were screened; citation-led searches then added foundational reports, methodological standards and conference records. The retrieved set contained many irrelevant diagnostic, nonmalignant and non-prostate studies, so the search count is not an included-study count. Of the records carried forward, 41 are cited here: eight single-compound animal tumor-burden comparisons, a set of combination studies with animal data, several pharmacokinetic and biodistribution reports, cell-only studies, methodological standards, two conference abstracts, a nanoformulation phase 1 trial, and clinical and mechanistic context, including human prevention and standard-of-care trials. The quantitative comparisons, combinations, pharmacokinetic reports and the reporting audit are tabulated where each is discussed below.

This is a structured critical review, not a registered systematic review with independent duplicate screening. Its distinguishing feature is a verification step: every numerical value reproduced in the text, tables and figures was checked against the primary full text wherever that text could be retrieved, and the two places where a previously reported discrepancy could not be substantiated from the source are identified explicitly below. Reporting was audited against the domains of the SYRCLE risk-of-bias tool without assigning a numerical quality score {Hooijmans, 2014 #3}; the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines informed the expectations for animal numbers, exclusions, randomization, masking and endpoint reporting {Percie du Sert, 2020 #4}; and the MIRIBEL standard informed the expectations for formulation and bio–nano characterization {Faria, 2018 #5}. No author correspondence, exhaustive multi-database search, or prospective protocol registration was undertaken, so absence from the retrieved set is not evidence that a study does not exist.

The animal was treated as the experimental unit. The preferred estimand was the formulation-to-free ratio of mean terminal tumor volume at a common, prespecified time. Matching required the same active moiety, the same active-equivalent dose, the same route, the same schedule, the same treatment-start condition and the same endpoint. Separate strata were retained for dose-sparing designs, combinations, carrier-active systems and pharmacokinetic studies without a tumor outcome. The heterogeneity and extraction uncertainty uncovered during verification were sufficient that a pooled effect was not calculated; that decision, and the data that would make pooling defensible, are developed in the quantitative section below.

# What constitutes a fair formulation comparison

Equal nanoparticle mass is not equal drug dose (Figure 1A). Dosing must be expressed as measured active compound per kilogram or per animal, with drug loading and residual unencapsulated compound reported. A 100 mg/kg particle dose at 2% loading contains approximately 2 mg/kg of active compound, and nominal loading cannot substitute for measurement of the injected or gavaged preparation. Salts, prodrugs and covalent conjugates require molar active-equivalent accounting and evidence that the relevant species is released.

Route matching is equally decisive, because each route bypasses a different set of barriers (Figure 2B). Intravenous nanoparticles compared with oral free compound estimate the combined effect of route and formulation, not formulation alone. Intratumoral dosing probes local retention and activity but cannot establish oral bioavailability, circulation persistence or tumor targeting after systemic administration. The PLGA-curcumin study by Yallapu and colleagues makes the point concretely: the efficacy experiment randomized tumor-bearing mice to four groups of six and gave 25 µg of curcumin equivalent by intratumoral injection, whereas separate experiments assessed intraperitoneal toxicity and radiolabeled-antibody targeting {Yallapu, 2014 #2}. Merging those experiments into a single claim of systemic curcumin efficacy would exceed the evidence.

Vehicle matching is a third requirement that is frequently unmet. Free curcumin dissolved in DMSO or ethanol and nanoparticles suspended in buffered saline can differ in precipitation, local irritation and tolerability independently of any delivery effect. This is not hypothetical: in both principal intravenous and intratumoral curcumin comparisons the free compound was delivered in DMSO while the particles were delivered in phosphate-buffered saline {Yallapu, 2014 #2; Ma, 2019 #9}. A valid study needs the free-drug vehicle control and the blank carrier, each at the appropriate volume and schedule. Superiority to an unstable suspension can still be a useful pharmaceutical result, but it does not isolate tumor-selective delivery.

Two complementary comparisons should finally be distinguished. At equal administered dose, a formulation may improve exposure and efficacy, which is a legitimate delivery advantage. At equal plasma or tumor exposure, persistent superiority points instead to altered spatial distribution, intracellular release or carrier pharmacology. A dose-sparing claim is strongest when full dose–response curves show a reproducible leftward shift, not when a single low nanoparticle dose is set against a single high free dose. The stage of disease at which treatment begins is the last axis: treatment started within a day of inoculation measures suppression of engraftment and early outgrowth, whereas treatment started once tumors reach a prespecified volume measures control of established disease. Both designs appear below, and they are not pooled.

Siddiqui and colleagues treated 22Rv1 xenografts with intraperitoneal nano-EGCG at 100 µg per mouse or free EGCG at 1 mg per mouse, three times weekly, with seven animals per group. At day 45, the reported mean tumor volumes were 1,242 mm³ for control, 854 mm³ for free EGCG and 707 mm³ for nano-EGCG {Siddiqui, 2009 #1}. The nanoparticle-to-free ratio is 0.828, a descriptive 17.2% lower volume achieved at one-tenth the administered dose (Figure 3A). This is a genuine dose-sparing signal, but no equivalence or non-inferiority margin was prespecified, so similar-looking inhibition is not proof of equivalent efficacy. Two design features bear on interpretation and were confirmed on the primary text: the study was terminated when control tumors reached 1,300 mm³, so the endpoint was triggered by control growth rather than fixed in advance, and the dispersion plotted in the tumor-growth figure is standard error, not standard deviation.

Khan and colleagues tested oral chitosan-EGCG in the same model. Free EGCG was given at 40 mg/kg and nanoparticles at 3 or 6 mg/kg, five times weekly, beginning one day after implantation, with 12 mice per group and two flank tumors per mouse. At day 32 the reported mean volumes were 1,200, 514, 310 and 216 mm³ for control, free drug, low-dose nanoparticles and high-dose nanoparticles, giving formulated-to-free ratios of 0.603 and 0.420 (Figure 3B) {Khan, 2014 #6}. These are unequal-dose comparisons, and the two tumors within an animal must not be counted as independent replicates. Because treatment began immediately after implantation, the experiment sits closer to suppression of engraftment than to treatment of established disease. The full text of this report could not be retrieved during verification, so its values are reproduced as extracted rather than independently re-checked, and are flagged accordingly in Table 2.

The later targeted-EGCG study compared free EGCG with untargeted and prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA)-directed formulations in 22Rv1-bearing mice, in four groups of 12 treated five times weekly, with the control group formed from two subgroups of six receiving void nanoparticles or vehicle {Sanna, 2017 #7}. Endpoint mean tumor volumes were 2,685, 1,895, 1,230 and 1,073 mm³ for control, free EGCG, untargeted and targeted nanoparticles, and the corresponding tumor weights were 3.0, 2.1, 1.3 and 1.1 g. Expressed relative to control, the two endpoints track each other closely (Figure 3C), which strengthens the internal consistency of the result. Three reporting problems nonetheless constrain its external use, and all three were confirmed directly against the source: the results section states 100 µg per mouse per day while the methods state 100 mg per mouse per day, a thousandfold discrepancy; the methods do not state the administration route for any arm; and the statistics section reports standard error whereas the tumor-volume figure legend reports standard deviation for the same experiment. A fourth feature is a design limitation rather than a reporting one: treatment began 24 hours after inoculation, so this experiment shares the engraftment-stage limitation of the oral study. The one genuinely dose-matched contrast within the experiment, targeted versus untargeted nanoparticles, gives a ratio of 0.872, but PSMA-mediated delivery requires more than the nominal presence of a targeting ligand to be established, as the exposure and reporting analyses below make clear.

Lin and colleagues examined a chemically characterized catechin mixture from oolong tea in DU145-bearing mice. Thirty-six animals were allocated to six groups of six, and both extract and nanoemulsion were given at 10 and 20 mg/kg body weight by intraperitoneal injection every three days for a total of six injections, beginning only once the mean tumor volume reached about 200 mm³, which makes this the cleanest test of established disease among the matched comparisons {Lin, 2021 #8}. Reported tumor-weight reductions relative to control were 27.9% and 52.6% for the two extract doses and 48.3% and 77.5% for the corresponding nanoemulsion doses, implying formulated-to-extract tumor-weight ratios of approximately 0.717 and 0.475. Two further points matter here. The study included a paclitaxel positive control that reduced tumor weight by 90.8% and therefore outperformed the best nanoemulsion arm; a formulation advantage over an unformulated botanical extract is not the same as competitiveness with a standard cytotoxic agent, and positive controls of this kind are rare enough in the wider literature that this one deserves emphasis. The article also reports the paclitaxel figure as 90.8% in the results and 90.6% in the abstract, and expresses the animal doses in µg/mL in its abstract where the methods correctly use mg/kg body weight. These values are not pure-EGCG effects and are retained as extract-level evidence.

At the level of prevention rather than formulation, a one-year double-blind trial in 60 men with high-grade prostatic intraepithelial neoplasia found far fewer progressions to cancer with green-tea catechins than with placebo, 1 of 30 against 9 of 30 {Bettuzzi, 2006 #39}. This trial does not bear on the formulation question and cannot support a dose-sparing claim, but it establishes a credible chemopreventive signal for the parent compounds, which is the context in which nanoscale dose-sparing is most plausibly useful.

# Curcumin shows formulation-dependent gains and a meaningful negative result

The intravenous curcumin experiments are shown in Figure 4A and Figure 4B, and the route and model-dependence of curcumin results in Figure 4C and Figure 4D.


The 2014 PLGA-curcumin experiment used C4-2 xenografts randomized to four groups of six: free curcumin, PLGA-curcumin, a DMSO vehicle control and blank PLGA nanoparticles. Both active arms received 25 µg of curcumin equivalent intratumorally, and animals were evaluated seven days later {Yallapu, 2014 #2}. The formulation improved antitumor activity and altered AR, β-catenin and other tissue readouts. This is a matched local-delivery comparison with an appropriate blank-carrier arm, and its interpretation must remain separate from the radiolabeled PSMA-antibody experiments, which were delivered intraperitoneally and in which radiolabel accumulation measures the labeled component rather than intact curcumin. Because the efficacy arm is intratumoral, it occupies a distinct route stratum and cannot support claims about oral bioavailability or systemic targeting (Figure 4C).

Ma and colleagues compared free curcumin, untargeted liposomes and CD133-aptamer A15 liposomes at 25 µg of curcumin equivalent by intravenous administration in DU145-bearing mice, with six animals per group {Ma, 2019 #9}. The source table reports endpoint volumes of 365 ± 156, 275 ± 135 and 121 ± 72 mm³ for these arms against 659 ± 136 mm³ for blank liposomes, and, critically, day-1 volumes of 131 ± 22, 126 ± 28, 131 ± 31 and 129 ± 26 mm³. Treatment therefore began with established tumors of comparable size, and relative growth over the treatment period was 2.79, 2.18 and 0.92 for free curcumin, untargeted and targeted liposomes against 5.11 for blank liposomes. The targeted arm is the only one in this review whose mean endpoint volume falls below its own day-1 mean (0.92×), the nearest thing to regression in the series, though the within-group variance (121 ± 72 mm³, n = 6, no significance test, and the unresolved day-7 versus day-10 labeling) means it is not a demonstrated regression; the tumor weights of 0.325, 0.219 and 0.116 g against 0.625 g corroborate the ranking (Figure 4A). Reporting the baseline alongside the endpoint, as this study does, is good practice and makes the result far more interpretable than an endpoint volume alone. Two problems remain: the methods describe a seven-day assessment whereas the table heads its endpoint columns day 1 and day 10, so Figure 4A presents a source-reported endpoint rather than silently selecting either day; and the free drug was given in DMSO while both liposome arms were given in buffered saline, so vehicle is not matched.

Akanda and colleagues studied curcumin-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers in LNCaP xenografts, giving free curcumin and the formulation intravenously at 20 mg/kg twice weekly for 30 days {Akanda, 2023 #10}. Reported mean tumor volumes after approximately four weeks were 1,412.0 mm³ for untreated control, 1,375.2 mm³ for blank carriers, 1,155.1 mm³ for free curcumin and 778.4 mm³ for the loaded carrier, a formulated-to-free ratio of 0.674 (Figure 4B). Including the two control arms, which the primary report provides but the original manuscript omitted, materially improves interpretation: the blank carrier did not differ appreciably from untreated control, a result consistent with an inert carrier, so the gain is most simply attributed to delivery of the payload rather than to carrier pharmacology, although the two unreplicated group means cannot establish this firmly. Several reporting features nonetheless require clarification before variance-weighted pooling. The statistical section states that findings are means and standard deviation with n = 3, and no group size for the efficacy experiment is stated anywhere in the article; a value of six animals per group is sometimes assumed for this study but is not supported by the source. The formulation table lists the nominally blank carrier as containing the same quantity of curcumin as the loaded carrier, which must be a transcription error but is not corrected in the published table. The xenografts were raised in the fat pad of female nude mice, an unusual host and site for an androgen-sensitive LNCaP model that is worth stating when the result is cited. The study appeared online in 2021 and in its journal issue in 2023; it is one study, not two.

An essential counterweight is the Pten-deficient model studied by De Velasco and colleagues (Figure 4D). Dietary nanoparticle curcumin at 76 or 380 mg/kg/day for sixteen weeks did not reduce tumor burden, although the higher dose reduced cancer-cell proliferation at 12 and 16 weeks of age, and liver enlargement occurred despite no effect on body weight or spleen size {De Velasco, 2020 #11}. This study does not directly compare nanoparticle and free curcumin and cannot estimate their relative efficacy, but it shows that a favorable proliferation marker and apparently stable body weight can coexist with absent tumor-burden benefit and an organ-level safety signal. It should not be omitted because it complicates the formulation narrative. A separate line of work adds a further caution about attributing effects to passive encapsulation: a curcumin formulation carrying nitroxide radicals suppressed oxidative degradation and showed antitumor activity after intravenous administration, but here the carrier is deliberately pharmacological, and separating protection of curcumin from the carrier's own radical-scavenging requires a carrier-only arm and ideally a comparable carrier without the active redox groups {Thangavel, 2015 #12}.

# Quercetin, resveratrol and other natural products broaden the evidence, but not uniformly

Zhao and colleagues reported that DSPE-PEG2000 quercetin micelles improved aqueous solubility and cell-based potency, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration of 20.2 µM against more than 200 µM for free quercetin, and reported 52.03% tumor-proliferation inhibition relative to control in PC-3 xenografts treated at a quercetin-equivalent dose of 30 mg/kg {Zhao, 2016 #13}. That control-relative figure must not be relabeled as a 52% improvement over free quercetin. The dose and model are recoverable from the source, but numerical endpoint variance is not, so the study supports the qualitative evidence base without entering the matched-dose quantitative synthesis. Later CD44-targeted quercetin liposomes demonstrated activity in prostate cell lines and spheroids, but those experiments remain cellular rather than an independent animal confirmation {Turkekul, 2023 #14}.

Resveratrol illustrates how often a formulation paper stops short of an animal tumor outcome. Polymeric resveratrol nanoparticles built from a polycaprolactone and PLGA–poly(ethylene glycol) blend improved antiproliferative activity in prostate cancer cell lines but did not establish animal tumor control {Sanna, 2013 #15}. A solid-lipid resveratrol formulation reported prolonged disposition in rats and enhanced accumulation in a prostate-targeting assessment, but rat pharmacokinetics and cell-accumulation measures cannot be converted into a claim of xenograft efficacy without the relevant tumor-growth experiment {Sharma, 2022 #16}. Silica-encapsulated resveratrol was investigated for cellular antiproliferative effects and docetaxel sensitization under hypoxia, which is mechanistic support rather than a standalone animal effect estimate {Chaudhary, 2019 #17}.

Celastrol, also called tripterine, illustrates both delivery and comparator problems. A 2015 study of a polymer-blend formulation established activity in prostate cancer cells {Sanna, 2015 #18}. Yin and colleagues then tested intravenous tripterine nanoparticles at 1 mg/kg every other day in LNCaP-bearing mice, including an arm that received blank nanoparticles before the loaded particles to saturate reticuloendothelial uptake {Yin, 2017 #19}. The preloading strategy improved tumor delivery and antitumor activity, but it adds a second intervention, so the simple formulation contrast and the carrier-preloading contrast require separate estimates. Weight loss with free tripterine may reflect both compound toxicity and its propanediol–ethanol vehicle, and the report describes BALB/c mice without clearly establishing immunodeficiency in the examined passage, an unresolved point for a human xenograft model.

Recent cell-based work reinforces the need for carrier controls (Figure 5C). In a 2026 apigenin study, total apoptosis at a sub-half-maximal working concentration of 6.25 µM was 44.30% with hyaluronic-acid-coated selenium nanoparticles carrying apigenin, 31.36% with the selenium nanoparticles alone, and 9.77% with free apigenin {Vicil, 2026 #20}. The carrier is plainly not inert; it accounts for most of the effect attributed to the loaded particle, exactly the decomposition that a blank-carrier arm is designed to reveal. A second 2026 study combined luteolin, ciprofloxacin, lipid nanoparticles and light exposure in PC-3 cells, and its mechanistic claims belong to a combination and photodynamic framework rather than to in vivo efficacy {Alzhrani, 2026 #21}.

# Combination treatments require a different causal question

Co-delivery can synchronize tumor exposure to two agents and is a legitimate therapeutic objective, but it changes the comparison that is needed. The necessary comparator is the same free combination at the same active-equivalent doses, ratio, route and schedule; comparing a two-drug nanoparticle only with a free single agent or with chemotherapy alone cannot isolate the incremental contribution of formulation. The minimum design adds each free drug, their free mixture, each single-loaded carrier, the co-loaded carrier and a blank carrier where feasible, and a factorial layout is often more efficient than a large unstructured arm set. The combination studies reviewed here are summarized in Table 3.

Narayanan and colleagues reported reduced prostate adenocarcinoma in Pten-knockout mice treated with liposomal curcumin and resveratrol, together with increased curcumin exposure during coadministration {Narayanan, 2009 #22}. This is important prevention evidence, but formulation, compound combination and altered disposition are intertwined. Chu and colleagues evaluated EGCG and curcumin in a dual-targeted carrier in an orthotopic prostate model, which strengthens anatomical relevance while introducing two compounds and two targeting components {Chu, 2019 #23}. Neither design should be merged with pure-compound, subcutaneous, matched-dose experiments as though the interventions were interchangeable.

The taxane-containing hybrids address a different and clinically grounded question, because docetaxel is standard of care in metastatic disease {Tannock, 2004 #40}. Curcumin–docetaxel lipid–polymer hybrids produced favorable xenograft findings {Yan, 2016 #24}; aptamer-functionalized curcumin–cabazitaxel hybrids showed antitumor activity {Chen, 2020 #25}; and resveratrol–docetaxel liposomes, compared with a liposome-free combination, were associated with tumor inhibition and longer survival in the reported PC-3 experiment {Zhang, 2022 #26}. Each addresses a particular regimen, and without complete dose and variance extraction their positive findings cannot establish the size of a general formulation effect or the independent contribution of the natural product. Silibinin–cabazitaxel liposomes initially supplied cell-based CD44-targeting evidence {Mahira, 2019 #27}, and a 2025 nanoemulsion study extended the combination to animals, reporting tumor-volume and tumor-weight reductions of 78.90% and 80.00%; the abstract-level figures do not provide everything needed to calculate a matched nano-to-free contrast with uncertainty {Almutairy, 2025 #28}. Genistein–plumbagin liposomes inhibited prostate xenograft growth, but a control-relative percentage is not a direct estimate of the contribution of encapsulation {Song, 2020 #29}.

The curcumin–piperine PLGA study by Yakubu and colleagues is mechanistically distinctive because it examines prostate cancer cells alongside steroidogenic assays in NCI-H295R adrenal cells and points to modulation of CYP17A1 and CYP21A2 (Figure 2D) {Yakubu, 2025 #30}. Its cell-viability, migration and steroid-metabolite findings justify further investigation, but the adrenal assays do not establish intratumoral androgen suppression in an animal, piperine itself is bioactive and alters drug handling, and reduced cortisol deserves evaluation as a potential endocrine liability rather than automatic classification as a desirable anticancer effect. These limitations apply equally to work from any laboratory.

# Exposure must connect delivery to tumor pharmacology

Higher solubility is a pharmaceutical improvement, not proof of higher oral systemic exposure, and cellular fluorescence is evidence of a fluorescent species, not necessarily of intact parent compound in the relevant compartment. A fluorescent payload can dissociate from its carrier, and degradation can alter signal intensity, so imaging must be paired with chemically specific assays that distinguish parent drug, metabolites and, where feasible, released from carrier-associated drug (Figure 5B). The pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies available for these compounds are summarized in Table 4.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Curcumin · A15-aptamer liposome | mouse · plasma + tissues (10 mg/kg i.v.) | tumor AUC₀–₁₂ₕ 1032 / 1484 / 4356 ng·h·g⁻¹; plasma 2306 / 1834 / 1475 | tumor rises 4.22× as plasma falls; index 3.12 (prose) vs 4.2 (table) | separate experiment from efficacy (25 µg); n and window conflicts | {Ma, 2019 #9} |

The A15-curcumin study provides the most instructive numerical example (Figure 5A). Its table reports tumor exposure (area under the curve over 0–12 hours) of 1,032.3, 1,484.3 and 4,356.3 ng·h/g for free curcumin, untargeted and A15 liposomes, with corresponding plasma values of 2,305.5, 1,833.5 and 1,475.0 ng·h/g {Ma, 2019 #9}. The targeted-to-free tumor ratio is 4.22 while plasma exposure falls, and this divergence between compartments is the most informative feature of the dataset: it is the pattern expected of redistribution toward the tumor, not of a simple increase in systemic exposure, although it rests on total-homogenate exposure that cannot by itself separate carrier-bound from released drug (Figure 5B). Three inconsistencies nonetheless limit its use, all confirmed on the source: the prose reports a targeting index of 3.12 against the 4.2 ratio printed in the same table; it describes a 24-hour accumulation window against the table's 12-hour heading; and the table states n = 6 while the methods describe three animals sampled at each of four time points. The biodistribution experiment also used 10 mg/kg whereas the efficacy experiment used 25 µg per animal, so these are separate experiments that do not directly establish exposure mediation at the efficacy dose.

A convincing pharmacokinetic study should report dose-normalized plasma exposure, tumor exposure and the tumor-to-plasma ratio, together with liver, spleen and kidney distribution (Figure 5D). For oral products, absolute bioavailability requires a suitable intravenous reference, and a higher oral exposure alone establishes only relative exposure. For injected products, concentration immediately after administration may mainly reflect circulating encapsulated drug. Measurement of intratumoral unbound parent compound is technically difficult but more informative than total homogenate content when the central claim concerns target engagement. Active targeting requires particular restraint: comparing targeted and untargeted carriers that differ in size, loading or release does not isolate receptor targeting, and receptor-positive and receptor-negative models, ligand competition, and measurement of intact payload are the complementary tests. PSMA or CD44 expression in a single cell line is not a guarantee of adequate expression throughout human disease, and carrier uptake by macrophages or stroma can raise bulk tumor signal without raising delivery to malignant cells.

# Tumor burden and toxicity should be interpreted together

Caliper volume, tumor weight, longitudinal growth, histological burden, metastasis and survival are related but distinct outcomes. A smaller endpoint tumor can reflect delayed engraftment rather than regression of established disease, and regression requires a decrease from the animal's own pretreatment burden; among the experiments reviewed here, the A15-liposome arm is the only one for which both baseline and endpoint volumes are reported and the endpoint mean falls below baseline {Ma, 2019 #9}. Reporting baseline and endpoint volumes together, as that study does, is the minimum needed to tell the two apart. Lower prostate-specific antigen (PSA) can reflect direct AR-pathway suppression or altered secretion rather than proportional tumor-cell killing, so PSA and Ki-67 should corroborate, not replace, tumor measurements; where both volume and weight are reported, as in the targeted-EGCG experiment, their agreement should be shown rather than reduced to a single number {Sanna, 2017 #7}.

Body weight is an insensitive safety measure, especially when a growing tumor contributes appreciably to total weight. Short studies in immunodeficient mice cannot establish the absence of delayed hepatic, renal, hematological, immune or reproductive toxicity, and claims of "no toxicity" should be replaced by the narrower statement that no toxicity was detected in the measures and interval studied. The liver enlargement in the Pten-curcumin study is the cautionary example {De Velasco, 2020 #11}, and the tripterine experiment shows the complementary point that a formulation may reduce toxicity by avoiding an injurious solvent even as greater delivery of the active compound creates a different toxicity pattern {Yin, 2017 #19}. For endocrine-directed formulations, adrenal steroid output and circulating glucocorticoid and androgen profiles should be monitored alongside tumor outcomes, because lowering a steroidogenic endpoint is not automatically selective prostate cancer targeting {Yakubu, 2025 #30}.

The one human nanoformulation dataset makes the stakes concrete. A phase 1 study of liposomal curcumin, conducted across advanced cancers rather than as a prostate-specific efficacy comparison, treated 32 patients {Greil, 2018 #31}. No dose-limiting toxicity occurred in 26 patients at 100–300 mg/m² infused over 8 hours, but among six patients at 300 mg/m² over the shorter 6-hour infusion, one developed hemolysis and three others had hemoglobin decreases exceeding 2 g/dL without hemolysis. Of eight patients assessed at 8 weeks by RECIST, all had progressive disease; of 15 assessed between weeks 4 and 8, 14 had progressive disease and one had stable disease, and no objective response was observed. This does not invalidate animal formulation research, but it refutes any assumption that natural origin or liposomal delivery guarantees innocuous treatment, and it shows that formulation-specific infusion conditions, not merely dose, determine the toxicity that emerges.

# Quantitative synthesis and why no pooled animal estimate is justified here

The within-study contrasts can be stated transparently, and they are plotted together in Figure 6C. For EGCG, the terminal-volume ratios of nanoparticle to free compound are 0.828 in the 2009 intraperitoneal experiment, 0.603 and 0.420 for the two oral chitosan doses, and 0.649 and 0.566 for untargeted and targeted EGCG in the 2017 report; the corresponding tumor-weight ratios in the last experiment are 0.619 and 0.524, close enough to the volume ratios to indicate that the choice of endpoint is not driving the result. For the intravenous curcumin experiments, the endpoint-volume ratios are 0.753 for untargeted liposomes and 0.332 for A15 liposomes against free curcumin, with tumor-weight ratios of 0.674 and 0.357, and 0.674 for the nanostructured lipid carrier against free curcumin. For the catechin nanoemulsion the tumor-weight ratios are 0.717 and 0.475. These are descriptive ratios of reported group means, and the dependence structure matters: the two nanoparticle arms in a multi-arm experiment share a free-drug group and are statistically dependent.

These ratios must not be ranked as though they came from one experiment. They span different compounds, carriers, models (22Rv1, DU145, LNCaP), host sex and implantation site, doses, durations, targeting strategies and vehicles, and they mix volume and weight endpoints. The reporting barriers that block pooling are documented study by study in Figure 6A and Table 5, and the conditions that would have to hold before pooling is defensible are enumerated in Figure 6B. If sufficient comparable data become available, the appropriate continuous effect measure is the log response ratio, lnRR = ln(mean nanoparticle / mean free), whose approximate sampling variance for independent groups is the sum over arms of SD²/(n·mean²); exponentiation returns a ratio in which values below one favor formulation for tumor burden (Figure 6D). Standard error must first be converted to standard deviation using the correct animal count, which in turn requires that the correct animal count be reported; tumor incidence requires a risk or odds ratio; time-to-event outcomes require a separate survival analysis; and repeated tumor measurements must not be entered as independent studies. A future synthesis should stratify by compound, route, established versus implantation-stage treatment, carrier-active versus carrier-inert design, and single versus combined payload, because random-effects pooling cannot repair incompatible estimands. With few independent studies per stratum, heterogeneity and funnel-asymmetry estimates would be unstable, so no funnel plot, pooled diamond or artificial precision is supplied here.

# Reporting bias, verification, and conference evidence

Positive cell studies are easier to publish than null animal studies, and favorable endpoints can dominate titles and abstracts. The nanoparticle-curcumin Pten report is instructive because its abstract emphasizes possible chemopreventive activity despite unchanged tumor burden {De Velasco, 2020 #11}, and the catechin nanoemulsion study included a paclitaxel positive control that outperformed every nanoemulsion dose, a comparison easily lost when only the formulation's control-relative reductions are quoted {Lin, 2021 #8}. This review does not attempt to quantify publication bias, because the retrieved, heterogeneous set is insufficient for a credible statistical estimate of missing studies. Within-study reporting concerns include unexplained exclusions, incomplete animal flow, unblinded caliper measurement, selection of a favorable time point, and counting wells or tumors rather than independent animals. In the inspected full texts, statements that animals were randomized often lacked sequence-generation or allocation-concealment detail; a missing description warrants an unclear assessment, not an assertion that randomization was absent. The verification pass undertaken for this review confirmed each of the dose-unit, endpoint-day and variance-type discrepancies described above directly against the primary texts. It also tested two concerns that could not be substantiated: a reported disagreement between the methods and results of the catechin nanoemulsion study on injection count was not present, the article consistently stating six injections {Lin, 2021 #8}; and the nanostructured-lipid-carrier study states no group size for its efficacy experiment, so the figure of six animals per group sometimes cited for it should not be assumed {Akanda, 2023 #10}. Published unit, timepoint and variance inconsistencies are extraction barriers, not in themselves evidence of misconduct, and a reviewer's inability to confirm a discrepancy is likewise not evidence that the underlying report is sound.

Conference evidence is distinct from full journal evidence. An AACR 2022 abstract described engineered thymoquinone nanoparticles against drug-resistant prostate cancer {Singh, 2022 #32}, and an AACR 2023 abstract described PSMA-directed planetary-ball-milled thymoquinone particles, alone or with docetaxel, in patient-derived and intratibial models {Singh, 2023 #33}. These records broaden the landscape but lack the complete methods, animal flow and numerical outcomes required here, and they were excluded from quantitative comparison; their relationship to each other and to later full reports cannot be assumed, and they should not be counted as independent animal replications without cohort verification. A related peer-reviewed thymoquinone report established activity against hedgehog signaling in docetaxel-resistant cellular models {Singh, 2020 #34} but does not by itself validate the animal claims of the later abstracts. Resveratrol-functionalized radioactive gold nanoparticles produced tumor control after intratumoral administration, but this is a radionuclide-containing therapeutic platform rather than an isolated test of resveratrol delivery {Sakr, 2025 #35}, and classifying it otherwise would exaggerate the evidence for natural-product efficacy.


# The next decisive experiments

A practical design that would resolve most of the ambiguities catalogued above is set out in Figure 7. First, select a chemically defined compound and a reproducible carrier with independently verified active loading, release and stability, and test the free compound, its vehicle, a blank carrier and the loaded carrier at matched active doses and routes; for targeted particles add a composition-matched untargeted comparator, and for two-drug systems add a dose- and ratio-matched free mixture (Figure 7A). Predefine which comparison tests delivery, which tests targeting, which tests combination benefit and which tests toxicity, because a single comparison against untreated controls answers none of these questions (Figure 7B).

Second, use a dose range that includes submaximal effects rather than only a maximally cytotoxic condition. Randomize after tumors reach a prespecified size, conceal allocation where feasible, and blind tumor measurement and pathology. Report every randomized animal, the endpoint denominator, every exclusion and every humane endpoint, and state the group size for each experiment rather than only a generic n in a statistics paragraph. Record baseline tumor volume at randomization as well as endpoint volume, so that growth inhibition and true regression can be distinguished. Analyze the animal as the unit, account for cages and multiple tumors, and match host sex and implantation site to the biology of the chosen line (Figure 7C).

Third, link efficacy to chemistry and exposure. Quantify parent compound and relevant metabolites in plasma and tumor at the actual efficacy dose, report tumor and plasma exposure together rather than tumor alone, and pair exposure with target engagement and with measurements that distinguish tumor cells from stromal or immune uptake. Where efficacy improves, perform an exposure-matched experiment or model to separate a greater delivered amount from altered intratumoral action, and include a treatment-free recovery interval to test persistence, recurrence and delayed toxicity.

Fourth, progress beyond one subcutaneous xenograft. Replication should include a biologically distinct AR-driven model, a model representing the claimed resistant phenotype, and an orthotopic, patient-derived or genetically engineered context chosen for the mechanism, and it should benchmark the formulation against the clinical standard of care rather than only against an untreated or free-compound arm (Figure 7D). PC-3 and DU145 are useful AR-poor models but do not represent all castration-resistant disease, and starting therapy immediately after implantation while describing the outcome as treatment of established disease should be avoided.

Finally, make the evidence auditable. Release animal-level tumor trajectories, raw exposure measurements, batch characterization and a prespecified analysis plan; report null tumor-burden results, toxicity and failed formulations; and reconcile every number that appears in more than one place in a manuscript, because unit, endpoint-day and variance-type conflicts between an abstract, a methods section, a results passage and a table were the single most common barrier to synthesis encountered here. A reproducible benefit from a modestly complex particle may be more translatable than a spectacular result from a poorly controlled multi-component system, and manufacturing reproducibility, sterile formulation, storage, administration burden and feasible exposure should be assessed before clinical promise is assigned.

# Conclusions

Nanoparticle formulation improves the measured activity of selected natural products in selected prostate cancer models. The clearest evidence supports pharmaceutical delivery advantages and several formulation-specific tumor-control signals, including one intravenous curcumin experiment in which the targeted arm's mean endpoint volume fell below its own mean baseline and a separate biodistribution experiment showed a pattern consistent with redistribution of drug toward the tumor. It does not support a single class-wide efficacy gain. Dose-sparing EGCG studies, matched curcumin experiments, active-carrier systems and multi-drug nanoparticles answer different questions and cannot be averaged into one. The negative tumor-burden finding in Pten-deficient mice, the superiority of a conventional taxane over the best nanoemulsion arm in the one study that included such a control, and the reporting discrepancies confirmed in several positive studies materially limit confidence. A defensible animal meta-analysis will require better comparator matching and the recovery or clarification of endpoint timing, variance and experimental units. Until then, transparent within-study comparisons, each traceable to a verified primary source, provide a sounder basis for interpretation than a pooled effect assembled from incompatible experiments.
